About

I am an AxPo Postdoctoral Fellow at Sciences Po. My research, at the intersection of comparative political economy and political sociology, examines, on the one hand, the formation of attitudes towards inequality and redistribution, and, on the other hand, how issues of conflict and political violence affect the perception of institutions and of that violence itself. Prior to completing the DPhil in Sociology at the University of Oxford, I earned an MSc in Economics at LSE and an MA in Social Sciences at Juan March-UC3M.

Research

Lady and the Tramp: Cross-class Romantic Partnerships and Attitudes towards the Economy

Abstract

Does having a partner from a different social class shape how individuals think about the economy? Drawing on longitudinal data from the British Household Panel Survey, which addresses concerns about selection on prior economic attitudes, and using multiple estimation strategies, I find that individuals who marry or begin to cohabitate with someone from a different class shift their economic attitudes to the left relative to those who partner within their own class. This effect can hardly be explained by changes in material conditions or by partner persuasion. The pattern is consistent with contact between social classes, which may reveal existing privileges and material differences between social groups. Consistent with this argument, I find no effect on political attitudes unrelated to the economy. Heterogeneous analyses indicate that selection and contextual effects vary by political interest, with contextual effects concentrated among less politically engaged individuals.

Hit me one more time? Civilian Victimization during Civil Wars and Attitudes towards the Police

Abstract

Building on research showing that exposure to violence creates citizens eager for stability, peace, and security, I argue that civilian victimization during civil wars is positively—not negatively—associated with attitudes towards the police, perceived as necessary for restoring order and stability and, thus, for preventing civil war recurrence. I test this theory for the cases of the Spanish and Guatemalan Civil Wars, finding that citizens living today in municipalities where civilians were more victimized by Francoist and Guatemalan state forces, respectively, hold more positive views of the police. Analyses of attitudes towards different security forces in Spain are consistent with this effect operating through institutional continuity with the perpetrator of victimization. Finally, I examine repression outside a civil-war context (scope condition), in Chile, and find a negative rather than a positive association, as violence was unidirectional and therefore could not be perceived or presented as necessary to prevent further violence.

Mandatory Military Service and Support for Redistribution: A tale of Inter-class Contact

Abstract

Does contact between people from different social classes heighten support for policies aiming to reduce inequality? Limited evidence and endogeneity issues call for further investigation into the political effects of inter-class contact. I contribute to this field by examining an unexplored institution in the existing literature: mandatory military service. A difference-in-differences approach, focusing on Spanish cohorts close to the removal of conscription and with women as the control group, reveals that men born in conscripted cohorts are significantly more likely to support redistribution. I present placebo tests discarding that these findings merely reflect a differing trend between genders in a broader set of political attitudes. Furthermore, I rule out plausible alternative explanations and present evidence of a positive relationship between conscription and cross-class social networks among men.

Goals against inequality? Football as an instrument to address the effect of national attachment on welfare attitudes

Abstract

Does national attachment offer the necessary glue to promote solidarity towards co-nationals and so increase support for policies in defense of the welfare state? Although this argument has been developed by liberal nationalists at the theoretical level, its empirical tests should be taken with caution given the endogeneity concerns present in the relationship between national identification and welfare attitudes. The present paper aims to overcome this problem by combining an instrumental-variable approach with an unexpected event during survey design. I exploit a survey run in the middle of Iceland national football team’s landmark qualification for the World Cup in 2017 for its first time in history—the smallest country ever qualifying for this tournament. Taking temporal distance to the event as an instrument of national attachment, I find mixed results for the causal effect of national attachment on welfare attitudes, contradicting the findings of naïve OLS analyses.
